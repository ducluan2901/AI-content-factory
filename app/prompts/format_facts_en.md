Format: numbered facts list, the shape of the facts shorts that go viral on English-language TikTok and YouTube Shorts (US, UK, Canada, Australia viewers).

What the viral ones do, and you must too:
- They talk TO the viewer about the viewer's own life. Not "Octopuses have three hearts" but "You have one heart. The octopus at the aquarium has three." Tie each fact to something the viewer has seen, owns or does.
- The hook decides everything: viewers who swipe in the first 2 seconds never come back. It is one short sentence, at most 12 words, about something the viewer owns, sees or does, pointing at a concrete thing the camera shows: "Your dog is telling you three things. Number three is right on its face." or "There is a hole in your eye, and your brain hides it every day." Never "Did you know", never a greeting, never an announcement about a faraway animal ("Bees hide three secrets").
- The first fact lands within 3 seconds: no build-up scene between the hook and fact 1.
- Keep it short: 2 or 3 facts in total, the whole video about 20 seconds.
- Every fact has three beats: the claim in plain words, one vivid concrete image that makes it real (a size, a comparison, a scene the viewer can picture), and a short twist or consequence ("so next time it stares at the wall, don't panic").
- The strangest fact comes last, introduced with a beat of suspense ("And number three is the weird one.").
- Sentences vary: mostly short, now and then one longer sentence for the vivid image. Never repeat a filler or reaction ("Seriously", "Wait") in the same script; use at most two reactions in total.
- The ending turns the viewer into a participant: a question they can answer from their own experience ("Does your cat do this?"), not "follow for more".

Topic choice (what the Vietnamese channel's data says: a dog's eyebrow muscles and the octopus's three hearts got 1,200 views each; bees, whales and sleep stages got 3 to 8):
- Pick a subject the viewer can check on themselves or on a pet right now (a dog's eyebrows, their own blind spot, a cat's tail), or a body part with a strange number (octopus: 3 hearts, blue blood). Abstract subjects (sleep stages, whale songs, the ocean) lose, because stock video cannot show them.
- Before writing, ask: can I film every scene with a common stock clip? If a fact needs a clip stock does not have, swap the fact for one that does.

Hook patterns to rotate (pick the one that fits, do not reuse the same pattern as the previous video):
- Number contrast: "You have one heart. An octopus has three." (a count or size the viewer can compare with themselves)
- Pointing at the viewer's own thing: "Your dog is telling you three things. Number three is on its face."
- Something they have never seen: "You have never seen this hole, even though it is inside your eye."
- A small test they can do in 3 seconds: "Close one eye and look at this dot." (only when the scene can show it)
- A common belief turned over, only when a reference work backs the correction.

Retention (one open loop, paid at the end):
- The hook plants a loop ("Number three is right on its face") and the last fact pays it. Do not pay it early, and say "at the end" or "number three" once so viewers stay.
- Between facts, one 4-word bridge that promises the next one ("Number two is stranger."). No recap, no thanks, no goodbye.
- The final line is the comment bait and doubles as the loop back to the first second, so a rewatch feels natural. It asks for a number, a yes/no or a name (e.g. "Which of the three does your dog do?"), never a generic "what do you think".
- When the topic ends with "Next: <subject>", add one closing sentence after the question, "Tomorrow: your cat.", so viewers have a reason to subscribe. Otherwise end on the question.

Title (the channel is one series, so every title starts the same way): "3 things your <subject> is telling you" or "3 things your <subject> is hiding from you", then a colon and the tease, at most 60 characters in all. Example: "3 things your dog is telling you: number 3 is on its face".

Language: plain spoken American English, contractions welcome, no idioms a non-native viewer would miss, no British-only or US-only slang. Use the unit the fact is usually told in (3 hearts, 20 times a minute).

Caption and hashtags (what English facts shorts with 1M+ views use):
- caption is one curiosity line that teases the strangest fact without giving it away, then the question for the comments. No paragraph.
- hashtags are exactly these, in this order: #didyouknow #facts #learnontiktok, then one tag for the topic in lowercase (#octopus, #cats), then #animals when the topic is an animal, and #fyp last. Spell them exactly as written.

Visuals:
- Each scene gets its own visual_query showing that fact's image, and no two scenes share one. Pick things stock video really has: the animal or object in action, close-ups, everyday settings ("cat stretching", "cat eyes close up", "cat sleeping sofa"). If a fact is about an inside part (heart, brain, blood), show the whole animal doing something instead.
- Name the right species in every query: a video about human eyes must say "human eye blink close up", never just "eye blink" (stock returned cats). Add "person" or "woman/man" for human subjects, and say what the camera sees in the last scene too: a still dark photo for the final 8 seconds makes viewers swipe, so ask for a moving clip ("person looking in mirror", "close up eye pupil"). Stock people may be of any ethnicity for this channel: do not add "asian".

Truth:
- State only facts a reference work states plainly. If something is disputed or unexplained, say so instead of resolving it.
- No invented numbers, names, dates, quotes or studies. Prefer a round, well-known figure over a precise one you cannot back.
- Never present a legend or a viral claim as proven ("nine brains" is a viral simplification, not a fact).
- sources is required: one line per fact, naming where a reviewer can confirm it, e.g. "Fact 1: Encyclopaedia Britannica, Cat entry" or "Fact 2: National Geographic, article on cat vision". A well-known publication or institution and the page or topic. Never a URL, because invented links look real.
